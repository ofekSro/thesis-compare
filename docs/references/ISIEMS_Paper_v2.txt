Abstract 
The ability to predict blast behaviour in an urban environment has gained significant interest in recent 
years. While blast wave propagation in free field can be predicted with reasonable accuracy using well-
established empirical and fast-running models, urban environments produce complex phenomena 
including channelling, shielding, multiple reflections and non-linear interactions between wave fronts, 
which limit the validity of such simple models. Three-dimensional Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) 
simulations capture these interactions in full, yet their computational cost renders them impractical for 
broad parametric studies or rapid real-time assessment. In recent years, advances in GPU-based CFD 
solvers have dramatically reduced simulation runtimes, making it feasible to run a vast number of high-
resolution simulations and enabling broad parametric studies that were previously impractical. 
This work presents a large-scale parametric study based on CFD simulations using ViperBlast, a 
GPU-based solver that enables a vast number of high-resolution three-dimensional simulations. The 
influence of key parameters is examined, including charge location, building dimensions, building 
density and the aspect ratio of building height to street width, on the pressure and impulse fields 
throughout the domain. 
Preliminary results establish an engineering prediction framework derived directly from the simulation 
database. For each pressure or impulse level characterised by the scaled distance in free field, a 
convergence radius was defined beyond which the loading in the urban environment may be assumed 
equivalent to free field. For the region where the influence of the urban environment remains dominant, 
two families of equations were fitted for the scaled distance in the urban environment and the 
corresponding physical radius, enabling rapid prediction of blast loads as a function of geometry and 
charge weight, and thus direct derivation of safety distances and expected damage. This framework 
serves as a fast and generalisable decision-support tool for protective design engineers without complex 
simulations for each scenario.

Introduction 
Explosive events in dense urban areas, whether by deliberate attack [1], industrial accident, or legacy 
ordnance recovered in built-up ground, are becoming more frequent. These events demand a 
capability to predict the blast loading they impose. This is required for the selective hardening of 
buildings and critical infrastructure, for safety distances in accidental events and in controlled 
demolition, and for real-time decisions such as evacuation and area closure. The charge weight and 
its location are uncertain in such cases, so an order-of-magnitude understanding of the pressures is of 
greater practical use than a precise value. 
Protection against explosive threats has traditionally rested upon free-field knowledge. The peak 
overpressure and the positive-phase impulse follow from the charge mass W and the standoff 
distance R alone, through the Hopkinson-Cranz cube-root law [2, 3]. The problem is thereby reduced 
to the scaled distance Z = R/W1/3, in m/kg1/3, and the relations of Kingery and Bulmash [4] express 
every free-field parameter as a function of Z alone. This description is one-dimensional. 
Within a built-up area, that description fails. The wave undergoes reflection from façades, 
diffraction about corners, channelling along street canyons, and shadowing behind blocks, and the 
resulting fronts superimpose non-linearly [5]. The geometry enters the definition of the load rather than 
correcting it, and points at equal scaled distance may sustain markedly different loads. Denny et al. [6] 
numerically reproduced the trials of Gajewski and Sielicki [7] behind a building corner. With the corner 
alone, the peak specific impulse at one gauge fell by 38% below the free-field value. Once a further 
wall was added to the simulation, the impulse instead exceeded that value by 169%, for the same 
charge standoff distance. The same reversal occurs at the scale of a whole array. Ratcliff et al. [8] 
report two layouts of identical areal density that differ only in the arrangement of the blocks, one 
reducing the central peak overpressure by 13.3% and the other increasing it by 35.5%, both relative to 
the case with no buildings present.  
A further departure arises where the charge lies within the street itself. The loading mechanism is 
then altered: lateral expansion is inhibited, the reflected fronts travel with the incident wave, and 
geometric attenuation is suppressed [9]. Hopkinson-Cranz scaling was found to hold in a wide street 
and to break down as the street was narrowed [10]. Ripley et al. [11] termed the regime in which the 
scaled-distance relations fail the extended near field. They also advanced one quantitative descriptor 
of enclosure, the degree of confinement. However, it correlates with the average scaled impulse only 
with appreciable scatter and was intended for ranking scenarios by severity rather than predicting 
load. A descriptor common to different urban configurations remains unavailable [8]. 
Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) resolves the governing physics, but each simulation returns 
the load field for a given specific geometry and charge, and not a relation that can be transferred to 
different cases. One of the fast-running models suggested by Hedhli et al. replaces the flow solution 
with an analytical superposition of unit waves [12]. Machine-learning surrogates infer the load from a 
database of computed cases [13]. Both surrender the explicit representation of the coupling between 
the geometry and the wave. Solvers accelerated by the graphics processing unit (GPU) have been 
developed in recent years. They can complete in minutes a simulation that requires hours on a CPU-
based code [14]. A parametric study of many configurations is thereby brought within reach. This 
paper reports such a study, conducted in ViperBlast [15] over an idealised periodic urban layout, 
together with the engineering prediction framework distilled from it. 
Methodology 
Idealised urban model 
The urban layout was idealised as a periodic array of identical rectangular buildings on a regular grid, 
as shown in Figure 1. Three lengths describe it completely: the square building footprint B, the street 
width s, and the building height H. The repeating unit cell is of side B+s. The buildings were treated as 
rigid, fully reflecting and impermeable obstacles. This is the prevailing representation in the urban 
blast literature, and it is conservative for the load channelled along a street [16, 17]. 
Two detonation scenarios were modelled. In the street detonation (det1), the charge lies mid-street 
between two parallel rows, feeding a single rectilinear canyon with two opposed propagation 
directions. In the intersection detonation (det2), it lies at a four-way crossing and feeds two orthogonal 
canyons with four such directions. The charge was specified as an equivalent TNT mass W, detonated 
at ground level.  
The charge occupies a point of two-fold symmetry in both scenarios, so one quadrant was 
modelled. The two symmetry planes and on the ground were fully reflecting, and the remaining

Numerical approach 
No single grid can resolve both the detonation and the far field at an acceptable computational cost. 
Each simulation was therefore advanced through a sequence of remapped stages, each solved in the 
lowest dimensionality its symmetry permits and supplying the initial condition of the next. The 
sequence comprised up to three stages. The detonation was first solved in one dimension, under 
spherical symmetry, until the wave reached the nearest façade. For street detonations in the narrow 
streets (s = 5 and 8 m), a two-dimensional axisymmetric stage followed, about an axis through the 
charge normal to the two façades. It carried the wave along the street until it reached the end of the 
block or the roof level. In all other configurations this stage was omitted. The solution was finally 
remapped onto the three-dimensional quadrant model, which was solved on three successive grids of 
increasing extent, the last reaching 500 m in plan. 
The models were solved in ViperBlast, a GPU solver integrating the compressible Euler equations 
explicitly on a structured Cartesian grid. The scheme is second-order accurate in space and in time. 
The air was treated as an ideal gas. The free-field predictions of the solver agree with Kingery and 
Bulmash to within approximately 10% in peak overpressure [18]. The impulse falls consistently some 
10% below the empirical curves, an offset attributed to the conservatism of the relations themselves 
rather than to the solver [18]. 
The three-dimensional cell sizes of 15, 50 and 100 cm were fixed by a mesh convergence study on 
the most demanding configuration. Tolerances of 10% on the peak overpressure and 5% on the 
impulse were met at between 95.4 and 100% of 632 monitoring points. The discretisation was held 
invariant across all configurations, and each reference run used the same grids as its urban run, so 
any residual bias is shared by the two members of each pair. This mesh convergence is a property of 
the discretisation and is not the convergence radius defined below.

Two of the parameters, ρ and H/s, are dimensionless. The scaled street width s̃ carries the units of the 
scaled distance, m/kg1/3. Each parameter corresponds to a physical mechanism. The plan-area 
density ρ is the built fraction of the ground plane, and it governs the reflecting surface the wave 
encounters. The scaled street width s̃ expresses the street width relative to the charge, in the same 
way that Z expresses the standoff distance. At s̃ = 1 m/kg1/3, the street width in metres equals the cube 
root of the charge mass in kilograms. This value is a candidate regime boundary between a street that 
channels the blast and one that blocks it. The canyon aspect ratio H/s measures the depth of the 
street channel. 
Several street widths were considered (Table 1), where the two extreme street widths were

simulation, defined by five inputs: B, s, H, W and the detonation scenario. A geometry denotes one 
combination of B, s and H. The design comprises a balanced core block of 72 configurations, in which 
every combination of two values of B, two of s, three of H, three of W and two detonation scenarios 
was simulated once (2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 2 = 72). The effects of the five inputs can therefore be separated 
from one another. 
A 24-configuration extension populates the interior of the ranges of the three parameters, with six 
further geometries at two intermediate charge weights in both detonation scenarios (6 × 2 × 2 = 24). 
The extension is not balanced, and it is excluded from the analyses that separate the effects of the 
inputs. The two blocks jointly comprise 96 configurations and span 18 distinct geometries (12 in the 
core block and 6 in the extension). Two configurations sharing the three parameters of Equation (1) 
are geometrically similar, and under exact Hopkinson-Cranz scaling their fields coincide when 
expressed in Z. For each detonation scenario, the four dimensional inputs B, s, H and W therefore 
reduce to three parameters.

Measured quantities 
Every urban run was paired with a matched reference run with identical stages, grids and parameters, 
but without the buildings, that is a free-field simulation of the same charge. A simulated reference was 
preferred to the empirical relations so that the comparison reflects the buildings alone. In both runs, 
the peak overpressure and the positive-phase impulse were recorded in every cell. The urban effect

Results and Discussion 
The Urban Field and the Free Field 
The measured quantities defined above were extracted for all 96 configurations. A comparison of the 
peak overpressure and the impulse shows that the urban layout affects the two differently, and this 
difference governs the form of every result reported below. The layout amplifies the peak overpressure 
in some configurations and attenuates it in others, depending on the street width. Over the 96 
configurations the two effects nearly cancel. The effect on the impulse does not cancel. It is an 
amplification, and it is present in almost every configuration. Expressed through the amplification 
factor of Equation (5), the median is 0.995 in peak overpressure against 1.261 in impulse. On 
average, the urban layout alters the peak overpressure far less than the impulse.  
The peak overpressure registers the single largest arrival. In most of the field this is the leading 
front. The later reflections are attenuated and usually remain below it, although in a small number of 
locations a later reflection was found to set the peak. In narrow streets the first reflections from the 
façades travel with the leading front and raise it, which accounts for the amplification of the peak 
reported below. The positive-phase impulse is the integration of the pressure, and every reflection 
contributes to that integral.  
Figure 2 presents the ratio fields P/Pff and i/iff for a narrow street and for a wide one up to 80 m 
from the charge. Three mechanisms recur. The wave is channelled along the street axis, its lateral 
expansion inhabited by the parallel façades. Shadowing develops behind the building blocks. Within 
the layout, the wave reverberates between successive rows, at a more modest excess that is 
sustained further. All three are pronounced in the impulse field. In the peak overpressure field, they 
are considerably more local. In both fields the urban influence weakens with distance, until the urban 
and the free-field values can no longer be distinguished.

The Convergence Radius 
The convergence radius defined above was obtained for all 96 configurations, separately in peak 
overpressure and in impulse, and the two radii differ. The median scaled radius Zconv is 8.99 m/kg1/3 in 
peak overpressure and 11.61 m/kg1/3 in impulse. At the median, the impulse radius is therefore about 
1.3 times the peak overpressure radius (11.61/8.99). This ratio depends on the tolerances adopted 
and is not a physical constant. This is a first engineering consequence of the different effect of the 
layout on the two quantities: free-field methods become applicable at a larger distance for the impulse 
than for the peak overpressure. Over all 96 configurations, the scaled radius in peak overpressure 
does not exceed 13 m/kg1/3. In impulse it exceeds 16 m/kg1/3 in two configurations only, and its largest 
value is 21.49 m/kg1/3. In peak overpressure this bound follows from the 10 kPa floor of Equation (2a), 
which the free-field overpressure reaches at a slightly smaller scaled distance. No such floor applies in 
impulse, and its radius extends farther. 
The five inputs were ranked by the size of their effect on the scaled convergence radius, using the 
core block, in which these effects can be separated. The street width ranks first or second in both 
peak overpressure and impulse. Its effect is best described through the scaled street width s̃ of 
Equation (1), that is, the street width relative to the charge, and not the street width in metres. In peak 
overpressure the charge weight ranks first. In impulse it acts mainly through its interaction with the 
street width, that is through s̃. 
The scaled street width also marks a threshold. Where the street is narrow relative to the charge, 
the canyon confines the wave and channels it along the street. Where it is wide, the blocks obstruct 
the wave instead, and where it is very wide the building height only weakly affects the peak-
overpressure radius. The transition lies near s̃ = 1 m/kg1/3, and it was measured rather than assumed. 
This value was proposed in advance, and the results are consistent with it. Below it roughly three 
configurations in four amplify the peak overpressure. Above it amplification ceases to be systematic, 
and where it persists it is weak. In peak overpressure, the effect of the canyon aspect ratio on the 
radius, measured by the slope dZconv/d(H/s), also reverses at this value: below it, taller buildings 
increase the radius, and above it they reduce it in nearly every case. In impulse, no such reversal 
occurs at any street width. Figure 3 presents the effect of building height on the radius and its reversal 
at s̃ = 1 m/kg1/3. 
The two quantities differ in a further respect. Among the three parameters of Equation (1), the 
impulse radius depends mainly on the plan-area density ρ, and the dependence is a smooth power 
law. A denser layout returns more reflection and gives a larger radius. In peak overpressure, the 
interactions between the inputs, that is their combined effects, are as large as the effects of each input 
alone, and no smooth power law describes the radius.

mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of the predicted radius remains below 10% in both quantities. 
The largest errors occur at the corners of the sampled ranges, where a withheld geometry has no 
neighbouring geometry from which to interpolate. A supporting test, with 500 random splits of the 
configurations, returned median errors of 8.8% in peak overpressure and 7.2% in impulse. In most of 
these splits, a withheld configuration has another configuration of the same geometry in the fitting set, 
so this test checks mainly the effect of the charge weight. 
The formulas interpolate within the ranges of Table 1 and are not to be applied outside them. A 
narrower range can be delimited, with ρ between 0.31 and 0.56, s̃ between 0.50 and 5.4 m/kg1/3 and 
H/s not exceeding 3. Within it every withheld prediction falls below 20% error, in both quantities. One 
further condition applies. The periodic description becomes representative only over several blocks. A 
prediction should accordingly be sought only at a radius exceeding roughly 1.5 unit cells, R ≥ 1.5(B+s). 
This condition can be checked in advance.

With Equations (6) and (7), the convergence radius, which bounds the urban influence, is 
predictable in closed form. The peak overpressure and impulse inside the radius are not yet 
predictable in this way. Closed-form equations have been fitted there and are undergoing LOGO 
validation. In terms of Λ and ξ of Equation (5), the amplification in peak overpressure is largest near

reflected fronts. In impulse no such dependence was found. Each configuration sets a single 
amplification factor that holds at every distance within the radius. 
Summary 
A parametric study of blast loading in an idealised urban environment has been reported. A total of 96 
three-dimensional CFD simulations were conducted in ViperBlast over a periodic array of identical 
rectangular buildings, each paired with a matched free-field reference run. The array was described by 
three parameters, the plan-area density ρ, the scaled street width s̃ and the canyon aspect ratio H/s. 
The charge weight enters the fitted relations through the Hopkinson length, so they may be applied to 
charge weights between those simulated. 
The principal finding is that the urban layout affects the peak overpressure and the impulse 
differently. The layout alters the peak overpressure only modestly on average, and it amplifies the 
positive-phase impulse in almost every configuration. The impulse is therefore the quantity most 
affected by the urban environment. 
The urban influence falls below the prescribed tolerances at a finite distance, the convergence 
radius. This radius is predictable in closed form from the three parameters, but two structurally 
different expressions are required. An additive form describes the peak overpressure and captures the 
reversal from channelling to blocking at s̃ = 1 m/kg1/3. A power law describes the impulse, in which this 
reversal does not occur. Under LOGO validation the mean absolute percentage error of the predicted 
radius remains below 10% in both quantities. 
The results are provisional and subject to several limitations. The predicted radius is a single value, 
whereas the urban influence varies with direction. The idealised layout is uniform and effectively 
unbounded, whereas real urban layouts are not. The coefficients are those of the tolerance of 
Equation (2) alone, and a different tolerance would change them. Work now proceeds on the loads 
within the radius, on the dependence of the amplification factor on direction, and on filling the corners 
of the parameter space that the present design sampled sparsely. Experimental validation remains the 
necessary complement to all three.
